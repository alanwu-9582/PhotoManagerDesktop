Create a poster. Each uploaded photo should be treated as a separate standalone artwork; do not combine multiple photos into a collage.

LOWER HALF — MODULAR GEOMETRIC DECONSTRUCTION

Extract the most recognizable elements of the original image, including the primary subject, silhouette, structural features, pose, spatial relationships, and narrative connections. Reconstruct them as a block-based, modular geometric deconstruction illustration.

Do not completely reproduce the photograph and do not create a realistic painted version of it. Remove irrelevant details and retain only the strongest visual memory points that communicate the subject’s identity, character, atmosphere, and relationship to the scene.

Rebuild the image through geometric simplification, overlapping shapes, layered forms, collage construction, and modular recomposition, so that the viewer can immediately recognize its relationship to the photograph above.

Emphasize block-like forms, modular structures, geometric segmentation, and large, complete areas of shape.

Avoid excessive fragmentation. Do not divide the image into numerous tiny, scattered pieces. Instead, construct the subject using fewer, larger, more complete geometric forms such as rectangles, squares, rounded rectangles, ellipses, triangles, and irregular solid shapes.

For human figures, facial forms may be simplified into square or angular shapes, with clearly defined jawlines and facial features represented through broad geometric planes.

For architecture, translate buildings into solid block-like masses, façade divisions, rhythmic window openings, structural transitions, and simplified architectural volumes.

Animals, plants, objects, landscapes, and other subjects should likewise be reduced into organized geometric forms based on a clear modular and block-based visual system.

Moderate exaggeration is allowed, but the result must remain mature, restrained, minimal, and sophisticated. Avoid overly complicated, childish, fragmented, or loosely assembled forms.

VISUAL LANGUAGE

Use a combination of:
flat color fields + collage composition + modernist geometric design.

The color shapes should feel like individually cut pieces of paper assembled into a deliberate composition, creating a clear, organized, tactile cut-paper collage effect.

Remove complex background details. Retain only a small number of essential shapes required to communicate the environment or narrative. The background must never become a secondary visual focal point.

COMPOSITION

Maintain a relatively small-scale primary subject surrounded by generous negative space.

Arrange the composition freely according to the subject’s direction, proportions, visual weight, center of gravity, movement, and structural flow.

The subject may be asymmetrically positioned, placed near an edge, partially cropped, or deliberately offset from the center.

Maintain a clear visual hierarchy, controlled density, and balanced spacing. Pay close attention to positive and negative shapes, the visual weight of large color fields, and the breathing space created by empty areas.

TEXTURE

Use a gentle paper grain with a subtle printmaking or art-print quality.

Color fields should retain delicate paper texture, soft grain, mild noise, and slight imperfections associated with traditional printing.

The overall material quality should resemble handmade paper collage, editorial art printing, or fine-art printmaking.

Avoid smooth digital vector surfaces, glossy rendering, 3D rendering, photographic filter effects, or excessive digital polishing.

COLOR PALETTE

Extract 2–4 of the most distinctive and atmospheric colors from the photograph in the upper half.

Refine these colors into a more sophisticated, coherent palette. They may be slightly brightened, purified, or desaturated as needed to create an elegant contemporary system.

Suitable tones may include:
cream white, apricot, peach pink, coral orange, misty blue, muted blue, gray-green, sage green, warm brown, soft lavender, and other light, modern, timeless colors.

The dominant colors should feel distinctive but never harsh or overly saturated. Supporting colors should be gentle, layered, and harmonious.

The overall palette should feel clean, warm, contemporary, fashionable, approachable, and refined.

Avoid muddy yellow, dirty gray, dark muddy brown, fluorescent colors, cheap candy-like colors, excessive saturation, and chaotic color combinations.

TYPOGRAPHY

Introduce only a minimal amount of typography. Any language may be used.

Based on the subject’s identity, location, atmosphere, emotion, symbolism, or narrative, derive a short title, small annotation, number, caption, or brief descriptive text.

Typography must remain small, light, subtle, and restrained. Position it within negative space or near the edges and corners of the composition.

The text should form a sophisticated editorial relationship with the geometric artwork rather than functioning as a dominant commercial headline.

OVERALL AESTHETIC

The final poster should establish a unified visual language through:

large-scale geometric deconstruction,
restrained collage construction,
soft yet sophisticated color palettes,
subtle paper grain,
generous negative space,
and refined editorial typography.

Whether the source image contains people, architecture, animals, plants, objects, or landscapes, always preserve a clearly recognizable identity while translating the subject into a coherent modular geometric system.

AVOID:
photorealistic illustration,
literal tracing,
excessive fragmentation,
numerous tiny geometric pieces,
childish collage aesthetics,
overly complex perspective,
dense details,
cartoon-like styling,
3D aesthetics,
generic poster templates,
glossy vector graphics,
over-polished digital rendering,
and cheap souvenir or mass-market cultural-product aesthetics.
